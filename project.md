Project iBetter 25: The Front Range Multi-Modal Corridor

Document Version: 1.0.4 (Final Draft)
Author: Chief Engineer Flench
Jurisdiction: Colorado Transit Authority & CDOT

1. Executive Summary

The Interstate 25 corridor is the economic spine of Colorado, yet decades of standard highway widening have resulted in induced demand, catastrophic bottlenecks, and a severely limited capacity ceiling.

Project iBetter 25 is a generational infrastructure overhaul replacing the traditional car-centric interstate with a high-capacity, multi-modal transportation trench. Utilizing a Copenhagen-style dual-tier quad-track rail system alongside dedicated general-purpose and HOV highway lanes, iBetter 25 is designed to move up to 84,000 people per hour bidirectionally.

Because the physical Right-of-Way (ROW) varies drastically across the state, this corridor is dynamically engineered to expand to a massive 250-foot profile in suburban/rural areas and compress into a tight 140-foot profile through historic urban cores, without sacrificing its core rail capacity.

2. The Architectural Cross-Sections

iBetter 25 relies on two primary cross-sectional designs to adapt to Colorado's diverse geography.

The 250-Foot "Suburban/Rural Max" Profile

Deployed between Pueblo and Castle Rock, and Thornton to Fort Collins.
This footprint utilizes the vast ROW available outside the urban core to maximize heavy freight, high-speed rail, and express toll lanes.

Rail (Outer Flanks): $10\text{-}12\text{ ft}$ outer walkways, $12\text{ ft}$ Local Track, $14\text{ ft}$ Island Platform, $12\text{ ft}$ Express Track (75 mph).

Safety Buffers: $8\text{ ft}$ TL-5 continuous concrete crash barriers isolating rail from highway traffic.

Highway (Center): 3 General Purpose Lanes ($11\text{ ft}$ each), $3\text{ ft}$ delineated buffer, 1 HOV/Express Lane ($11\text{ ft}$), $4\text{ ft}$ inside recovery shoulder.

Divider: $4\text{ ft}$ bidirectional center concrete median.

The 140-Foot "Urban Core Squeeze" Profile

Deployed through Central Denver (Valley Highway: Santa Fe Dr to 20th St).
Designed to avoid eminent domain and the destruction of central Denver neighborhoods, this "road diet" removes bypass lanes to protect the high-capacity rail lines.

Rail (Outer Flanks): $8\text{ ft}$ outer walkway, $10\text{ ft}$ Local Track, $10\text{ ft}$ Island Platform, $12\text{ ft}$ Express Track.

Safety Buffers: $6\text{ ft}$ concrete crash buffers.

Highway (Center): HOV/Express lanes are removed. 2 General Purpose Lanes ($11\text{ ft}$ each). Traffic speed naturally reduced to 55 mph.

3. Route Walkthrough (South to North)

The corridor stretches over 280 miles, dynamically shifting its configuration to meet localized demands while maintaining continuous intercity rail.

Zone 1: The Southern Gateway (Trinidad to Pueblo)

Configuration: Modified Rural.

Details: Emerging from the New Mexico border, the corridor acts as a traditional interstate but with a protected dual-track (intercity only) rail alignment on the western flank. South of Pueblo, local commuter rail is unnecessary, so the ROW focuses on high-speed rail integration and freight mobility.

Zone 2: The Southern Expansion (Colorado Springs to Castle Rock)

Configuration: Full 250-foot Suburban Max.

Details: As the corridor hits Colorado Springs, the Quad-Track system begins. The Air Force Academy and Monument stations act as major park-and-ride hubs.

The South Gap Sprint: Between Monument and Castle Rock, local train stations disappear. Local trains (S1 Line) shift to the outer tracks and accelerate to 75 mph, operating as express trains until they reach the Castle Rock hub.

Zone 3: The T-REX Commuter Zone (Lone Tree to Denver South)

Configuration: 250-foot profile transitioning to dense transit.

Details: This is the highest-ridership zone for the Metro Local (M1) and Southern Local (S1) lines. The massive 14-foot island platforms easily handle the surge of Tech Center (DTC) workers. Highway interchanges are heavily modified; all on-ramps and off-ramps are converted to elevated flyovers or tunnel underpasses to leap over the outer rail tracks.

Zone 4: The Valley Highway Squeeze (Central Denver)

Configuration: 140-foot Urban Road Diet.

Details: North of Alameda, the ROW drastically narrows. The highway drops to 2 lanes per direction. Acoustic glass walls top the crash barriers to protect waiting passengers from highway noise and exhaust. Denver Union Station serves as the master interchange hub where all local and express lines converge.

Zone 5: The Northern Reach (Thornton to Fort Collins)

Configuration: Returns to 250-foot Suburban Max.

Details: Pushing north past Boulder Junction and Longmont, the ROW opens back up. The Northern Local (N1) services the rapidly growing suburban sprawl, while the Front Range Express (FRX) blasts through at 75 mph, making the trip from Fort Collins to Denver Union Station in under 45 minutes. The corridor eventually scales back down to a dual-track rural layout as it approaches Wellington and the Wyoming border.

4. Transit Operations: The Hub-and-Spoke Zone Model

A 130-mile commuter train stopping every two miles is unrideable. iBetter 25 utilizes a zoned, cross-platform transfer model.

The Fleet & Lines

Front Range Express (FRX - Blue): The backbone. Heavy, aerodynamic commuter trains running continuously at 75 mph. Stops only at Fort Collins, Loveland, Denver Union, Castle Rock, Monument, and Colorado Springs.

Northern Local (N1 - Green): Articulated light-rail serving local stops from Fort Collins to Denver Union.

Metro Local (M1 - Purple): High-frequency urban rail cycling between Thornton, Denver Union, DTC, and Castle Rock.

Southern Local (S1 - Amber): Services Denver down to Colorado Springs, utilizing the high-speed "Sprint Zone" through the South Gap.

The Transfer Experience

Passengers traveling from a minor suburb (e.g., Berthoud) to the Springs will board the N1 Local. At Denver Union, they exit onto the shared island platform and walk 10 feet across the concrete to board the waiting FRX Express train. There are no stairs or concourses required to switch between local and express service in the same direction.

5. Engineering Challenges & Solutions

At-Grade Conflicts: Because rail is placed on the outer flanks (improving neighborhood pedestrian access), cars cannot cross the tracks at grade. Solution: 100% of highway exits utilize "Texas T" style flyovers or shallow underpass trenches.

Aerodynamic Suction: Express trains passing platforms at 75 mph create dangerous slipstreams. Solution: 14-foot platforms feature a marked 4-foot tactile danger zone and aerodynamic wind-deflection pylons.

Incident Recovery: A stalled car in a standard HOV lane creates severe bottlenecks. Solution: The 250-foot layout includes a dedicated 4-foot inside recovery shoulder, allowing the HOV/Express lane to flow uninterrupted even during minor collisions.
